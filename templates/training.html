1. Phishing Awareness

Learn to recognise suspicious messages, misleading links and fraudulent websites.

Read Lesson

What is phishing?

Phishing is a cyberattack in which criminals use deceptive emails, text messages or websites to trick people into revealing passwords, financial details or other sensitive information.

Warning signs

Attackers often impersonate trusted organisations, such as universities, banks or employers. They may claim that an account will be suspended unless the recipient acts immediately.

A message can display a familiar name or logo while directing you to a fraudulent website. On a computer, hovering over a link may reveal its real destination.

Be cautious with attachments. A filename ending in .pdf.exe is an executable program, not an ordinary PDF document.

How to stay safe

Open the organisation's official website independently or contact it through a trusted channel. Do not follow suspicious links.

If you have entered a password on a suspected phishing website, change it through the legitimate service and report the incident.

2. Password & Account Security

Protect your accounts using unique passwords, password managers and secure recovery methods.

Read Lesson

Strong passwords

Strong passwords should be long, difficult to predict and unique to each account. Avoid names, birthdays and simple patterns.

Reusing passwords is dangerous. If one website suffers a data breach, attackers may try the stolen password on your other accounts.

Password managers and passkeys

A reputable password manager can generate and securely store different passwords.

Some services offer passkeys, which use cryptographic authentication instead of traditional passwords and resist many common phishing attacks.

Account recovery

If a service warns that your password appeared in a breach, change it and check whether you reused it elsewhere.

Store account recovery codes securely and separately from your usual sign-in method.

3. Multi-Factor Authentication

Understand how MFA protects accounts and how to recognise suspicious login requests.

Read Lesson

What is MFA?

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) adds another layer of security by requiring additional verification when signing in.

For example, a user may need to approve a notification in an authentication app or enter a temporary verification code.

Unexpected login requests

MFA reduces the risk that a stolen password alone will allow an attacker to access an account. However, unexpected verification requests should never be approved.

Attackers may repeatedly send login approval notifications, hoping that the user approves one out of frustration. This is called MFA fatigue.

How to stay safe

Reject login requests you did not initiate. Check your account for suspicious activity and report repeated unexpected requests.

4. Social Engineering

Identify impersonation, manipulation and attempts to gain unauthorised access.

Read Lesson

What is social engineering?

Social engineering involves manipulating people into revealing confidential information, providing access or performing unsafe actions.

Attackers may pretend to be IT support staff, managers or other trusted individuals. They may use urgency, authority, fear or attractive rewards.

Common examples

Someone claiming to be from IT support might ask you to install remote-access software. Another attacker may impersonate a manager and demand confidential records.

Tailgating occurs when someone enters a restricted area by following an authorised person through a secure door.

Attackers may also build trust gradually, collecting small pieces of information that support a later attack.

How to stay safe

Verify unusual requests using trusted procedures. Never share passwords, recovery codes or confidential records simply because someone claims authority.

5. Malware & Ransomware

Learn how malicious software spreads and how to protect and recover important files.

Read Lesson

Understanding malware

Malware is malicious software that may steal information, damage files or interfere with computer operations.

Ransomware is a type of malware that can encrypt files or block access to systems and demand payment. Paying does not guarantee recovery.

How infections happen

Malware can arrive through suspicious downloads, unexpected attachments, compromised websites or unwanted software bundled with installers.

Security software may place suspicious files into quarantine to prevent them from running. Do not restore quarantined files without trusted guidance.

Backups and recovery

Regular backups help restore important data. However, backups permanently connected to an infected computer may also be encrypted.

Keep appropriately protected backup copies and test that files can actually be restored.

If ransomware is suspected, disconnect the affected device from networks where safe and report the incident promptly.

6. Safe Browsing & Email Security

Check websites, links, attachments and email recipients before sharing information.

Read Lesson

Website safety

Criminals can create convincing copies of legitimate websites. Always check the actual website address and organisation.

HTTPS indicates an encrypted connection, but a padlock does not prove that a website or seller is trustworthy.

Do not ignore browser warnings about invalid security certificates when entering sensitive information.

Suspicious links and pop-ups

Shortened links can hide their destination. When uncertain, visit the intended website independently.

Fake technical support pop-ups may claim that your computer is infected and tell you to call a number or install software. Close the suspicious page and use trusted security tools instead.

Email safety

Before sending an email, check the recipients. Choose Reply rather than Reply All when the information should not go to everyone.

7. Online Privacy & Personal Data

Reduce unnecessary sharing and protect your personal information online.

Read Lesson

Personal information risks

Information shared online can be misused for fraud, impersonation and targeted scams.

Public birthdays, schools, workplaces and pet names may help attackers create convincing messages or guess account recovery answers.

Privacy settings

Review social media privacy settings to control who can view your profile, photographs and posts.

App permissions

Applications may request access to contacts, cameras, microphones or location information. Only allow permissions genuinely needed for the application's purpose.

A navigation app may need your location while in use, but not necessarily when it is closed.

Data minimisation

Data minimisation means collecting or sharing only the information necessary for a particular purpose.

Question requests for personal details that do not seem relevant to the service.

8. Public Wi-Fi & Network Safety

Recognise fake hotspots and protect your information on unfamiliar networks.

Read Lesson

Public Wi-Fi risks

Attackers can create fake hotspots with names similar to those used by cafes, hotels or other public locations.

Confirm the official network name with staff or another trusted source. A strong signal does not prove legitimacy.

Automatic connections

Disable automatic connections to untrusted networks. Otherwise, your device may connect to an impersonated hotspot without your knowledge.

Protecting your information

Avoid unnecessary file sharing on public networks because other devices may discover shared resources.

Be suspicious if a public Wi-Fi login page unexpectedly requests your main email password or sensitive credentials.

Prefer trusted connections for sensitive work and use your organisation's approved security controls.

9. Device & Software Security

Secure your devices with updates, access controls and protection against data loss.

Read Lesson

Security updates

Operating systems and applications should receive security updates promptly. Automatic updates reduce exposure to known vulnerabilities.

A device that has not been used for several months may have missed important security patches. Update it before using it for sensitive activities.

Access permissions

Install software from trusted sources. Be cautious when applications request administrator privileges because these provide greater control over a device.

Protecting lost devices

Use a strong screen lock, PIN or password. Full-disk encryption protects stored information against unauthorised reading if a device is lost or stolen.

Device-location and remote-lock features can help protect information when a smartphone or laptop goes missing.
